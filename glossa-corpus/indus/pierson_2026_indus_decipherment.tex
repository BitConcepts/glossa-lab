\subsection{Abstract}\label{abstract}

We propose and test a falsifiable computational decipherment hypothesis
concerning the Indus Valley Script (IVS, ca.~2600--1900 BCE), which
remains undeciphered in the absence of a bilingual text. Working from the
Holdat LLC Indus Corpus V3 (1,670 seals, 9 sites, 390 distinct signs,
7,002 sign tokens; Mahadevan M-numbers), we apply positional analysis,
bigram/trigram collocational methods, DEDR rebus matching, and syllabic
language-model simulation to construct a candidate anchor model for IVS
sign readings. We find robust non-random positional structure
(\textit{z}\,=\,10.3; no permutation in 2,000 exceeded the observed statistic (p \textless{} 0.001; 0 of the 2,000 permutations exceeded the observed statistic, so the plain estimate is 0/2,000 and the standard add-one estimate is (0+1)/(2,000+1) = 0.0005, the smallest non-zero value a test of this size can report)),
a recurring three-slot positional pattern at the level of individual signs' positions. Whole inscriptions match the full pattern less often: in the Phase-133 check, 5.1\% of seals matched fully and 38.4\% partially (§3.31). The positions are measured; the reading of the three slots as classifier, title, and name/case elements of an administrative vocabulary is this model's interpretation, not a result of the positional tests (§3.2). We also propose 161 candidate readings at HIGH or MEDIUM confidence. These readings are assigned to signs that together account for 90.96\% of the corpus's sign tokens (69.8\% of seals consist entirely of such signs). `Coverage' here means only that the model assigns a candidate reading to a sign; it does not mean the reading is verified. Four of the 161 readings are provisional (see §2.2); without them the figures are 157 readings and 90.75\% of tokens. The corpus itself is not publicly released (see §6), so corpus-level counts cannot yet be re-run by other researchers. A fish-sign isolation count finds 0 isolated fish signs among the 113 fish-bearing seals in the 1,670-seal corpus, across all 9 corpus sites (§3.5). That count is not fish-specific evidence: the corpus contains no single-sign inscriptions of any kind (Phase-134, test F9), so no sign of any family can occur isolated in it, and §3.5 states the result at that strength. A separate search of the Gulf deposit catalog's text (Laursen 2010) found no fish-sign mentions at all, so it provides no counterexample to that pattern but also no confirmation of it; the two results count different units and are not combined (§3.19).
We correct a prior sign-counting error: an earlier count of 268 H+M signs
included 107 heuristic placeholders not meeting the stated evidence
criteria. Of the 75 HIGH-confidence readings, the reading strings of 44 (59\%) also appear in Parpola (1994), the work whose framework guided this analysis --- appearances in his text, not a sign-by-sign check that he proposed the same reading for the same sign (see §3.22); the
evidence is consistent with a Proto-Dravidian reading framework under the
tested assumptions, though Dravidian superiority over proto-Munda has not
been formally quantified. The program's internal consistency suite reported 59 of 59 checks passing at Phase-170 (May 2026); on the October 2026 execution tree the changed suite reported 40 passed, 0 failed, 8 warnings (Appendix C). These are internal consistency checks, not external validation. This study does not claim epigraphic finality; it proposes a falsifiable computational model whose method and repository artefacts (scripts, phase reports, and the anchor table) can be re-run and checked. The corpus-level counts in this paper cannot be re-run until the corpus itself is released; that limit is stated here, not only in §6, because most of the paper's numbers are corpus-level counts. The model's structural predictions and candidate phonetic readings can be tested against larger corpora and future archaeological discoveries. Its corpus-level counts cannot yet be re-run by other researchers, for the reason given above.

\subsection{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsubsection{1.1 The Indus Valley Script
Problem}\label{the-indus-valley-script-problem}

The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC, ca. 2600--1900 BCE) produced several thousand inscribed objects --- stamp seals, copper tablets, potsherd graffiti, and the Dholavira signboard --- bearing a script of roughly 400 distinct signs in the standard catalogues (this paper's own corpus attests 390 of them; §2.1). Despite a century of scholarship, the script remains undeciphered. The primary obstacles are: (1)
the absence of a bilingual text; (2) uncertainty about the underlying
language; (3) short average inscription length (\textasciitilde4--5
signs); and (4) limited corpus size relative to modern decipherment
corpora.

Competing hypotheses have proposed proto-Dravidian (Parpola 1994;
Mahadevan 1977), proto-Munda (Witzel 1999), an Indo-Aryan language
ancestral to Sanskrit (Jha \& Rajaram 2000 --- widely rejected), and a
non-linguistic semasiographic system (Farmer, Sproat \& Witzel 2004).
The Dravidian hypothesis is among the most extensively developed linguistic interpretations: the
geographic distribution of Dravidian languages, archaeological
continuity between IVC and later South Asian cultures, and a long tradition of applying the ``rebus principle'' (phonetic punning via sound-alike words) to IVS signs, beginning with Heras in the 1930s and developed most fully by Parpola. Individual rebus readings remain contested; none is treated as established in this paper.

\subsubsection{1.2 Prior Computational
Work}\label{prior-computational-work}

Rao et al.~(2009, \emph{Science} 324:1165) reported that IVS sign entropy falls in the range of known linguistic systems; in their own words, their results ``increase the probability that the script represents language'' --- a probability increase, not a demonstration. That entropy work has been criticised (Sproat 2014) and defended (Rao et al.~2010); the question remains contested, and this paper does not rely on entropy as evidence that the script is a language.
Mahadevan (1977) produced the standard sign concordance and M-number
catalogue. Parpola (1994, 2010) assembled the most comprehensive
Dravidian rebus decipherment. Wells \& Fuls (2015) produced an independent sign
list and catalogue. This paper's claim is narrower than any priority claim: it reports a candidate model whose assigned readings cover 90.96\% of this corpus's tokens, with the sign-by-sign evidence record held in the repository named in §6. No literature search for comparable coverage claims is on record, and none is claimed here.

\subsection{2. Data and Methods}\label{data-and-methods}

\subsubsection{2.1 Corpus}\label{corpus}

\textbf{Primary corpus}: Holdat LLC Indus Corpus v3 (Miller 2025). 7,002
sign tokens, 1,670 seals, 9 sites: Mohenjo-daro (n=606), Harappa
(n=492), Kalibangan (n=110), Dholavira (n=106), Lothal (n=124),
Chanhu-daro (n=78), Surkotada (n=61), Banawali (n=60), Rakhigarhi
(n=33). Sign encoding: Mahadevan M-numbers. This corpus covers the major excavated IVC sites. It consists of formal stamp seals from those sites; what share of the full published formal-seal assemblage its 1,670 seals represent is not measured in this paper, and no claim about that share is made.

\textbf{Sign catalogue}: 390 distinct M-number signs attested in the corpus; the anchor table assigns readings to all 397 signs in the Mahadevan catalogue (the additional 7 --- M086, M088, M090, M091, M092, M093, M094 --- have zero corpus tokens and are included for catalogue completeness). M-numbers are labels, not a count: the 397 catalogue entries carry numbers running from M001 to M416, with 19 numbers unused, so a sign numbered above 397 (for example M402) is normal. The full sign-by-sign inventory, with each sign's corpus frequency, is the file \texttt{ANCHOR\_STATUS\_AUDIT.csv} in the repository. Sign frequencies fall off steeply, from a few very common signs to many rare ones. An earlier version of this paper reported a Zipf exponent of about 1.7 for this distribution and described it as consistent with a natural language writing system. That exponent is this program's own statistic on this corpus; no derivation of it has been published that a reader can check, and we no longer present it as evidence that the script is a language. Yadav et al. (2010), who published a Zipf--Mandelbrot fit for Indus sign frequencies (on a pruned subset, the EBUDS subset, of Mahadevan's 1977 corpus), state the limit themselves: the presence of such a distribution ``is not sufficient to declare a sequence as linguistic''. A later test of what a fit of this kind can show is reported in §2.1.1 below.

\textbf{Crosswalk}: 25-entry Mahadevan\,--\,Parpola crosswalk
(mahadevan\_parpola\_crosswalk.json) for iconographic anchor validation.

\textbf{Supplementary sources}: Crawford (2001), used only for the Saar/Dilmun seal comparanda; Hojlund \& Abu-Laban (2012), used only for the Failaka pictorial-fish observation in §3.5; Parpola (1994, 2010), iconographic readings.

\subsubsection{2.1.1 What a Frequency-Curve Fit Can and Cannot Show (Spec 068)}\label{what-a-frequency-curve-fit-can-and-cannot-show-spec-068}

We tested what a frequency-curve fit can show, under rules fixed and registered before the test was run (Spec 068, executed as Phase-430; OSF registration 3pbwe). One fitting rule --- the Zipf--Mandelbrot rule frozen by Spec 053 --- was applied to Indus sign counts and to four comparison systems. The Indus counts came from a different corpus from the one described above: the Shakti corpus, a restricted corpus in the ICIT line (3,045 analysable rows of documented lineage; 11,805 tokens; 557 distinct signs). On those counts the fit gave exponent $\alpha$ = 2.004 and fit error 0.1584 (error is the root-mean-square difference in log space; smaller is a closer fit). On a wider set of rows from the same corpus (documented plus corroborated) it gave $\alpha$ = 1.96 and error 0.1544. The comparison systems were Mesopotamian numerical signs (error 0.5335), heraldic charges from the Dering Roll (0.1839), a synthetic emblem process (median 0.2405), and Linear B (0.9229). Linear B is the one system in the set that is certainly writing, and it fitted the curve worst of the five. Under the rule fixed before the run, that result makes the comparison UNINFORMATIVE: these measures do not separate the one certain writing system from systems that are not writing, so a close Indus fit on them is evidence neither for nor against the script being language. The Shakti numbers are a measurement of the Shakti corpus only. They are not a measurement of the Holdat corpus used in this paper, and they do not confirm or correct any earlier exponent, including the Phase-43 exponent of about 1.35 recorded in this program's files. \textbf{The four exponents mentioned in §2.1 and this section must not be compared with each other.} They were measured on different corpora, with different sign inventories and different fitting rules:

The full record of the test --- rules fixed in advance, input tables, and outcome --- is in this repository at \texttt{specs/068-yadav-zipf-controls-successor/} (see \texttt{outcome.md} and the \texttt{data/} tables), registered before the run at OSF: \url{https://osf.io/3pbwe}. \subsubsection{2.2 Evidence Hierarchy}\label{evidence-hierarchy}

Sign readings are assigned to three primary confidence levels, with PROVISIONAL\_MEDIUM used as a flagged subcategory of MEDIUM:

\textbf{HIGH (75 signs)}: Two or more of the following evidence criteria are met. The criteria are separate tests, but they are not independent of each other: all three are computed from the same corpus, and several from the same seals. (The iconographic criterion also draws on published Mahadevan and Parpola iconography and on DEDR for its rebus step; those are inputs to that criterion, not separate criteria in the list above.) - Iconographic match: the sign's shape is identified, following the published iconography of Mahadevan and Parpola, as depicting a known object whose Dravidian name yields the reading by the rebus principle (e.g., M045=yānai from elephant icon, DEDR 5175) - Distributional
exclusivity: sign appears on one and only one motif type with lift ratio
\textgreater{} 5.0 (e.g., M062=erutu exclusive to zebu bull seals) -
Terminal marker evidence: sign appears in \textgreater95\% of cases as
inscription-final, matching known Dravidian case suffix (M342=ay/ā,
genitive terminal)

\textbf{MEDIUM (86 signs)}: Single strong evidence source: - DEDR rebus match with SA-consistency \textgreater{}= 0.15 from syllabic language model simulation (SA: the syllabic language model fitted by simulated annealing, §2.4; for how many signs carry SA in their evidence, and the bound it implies, see §2.4)
- Positional profile matching a known morpheme class (INITIAL=title
prefix, TERMINAL=case suffix) - Supported allograph of a HIGH/MEDIUM
anchor by positional L1 distance \textless{} 0.2


\textbf{PROVISIONAL\_MEDIUM (4 signs --- M330, M165, M202, M198)}: Promoted from LOW on a narrower basis than ordinary MEDIUM: at least 3 text-proximity hits in the mined literature (Phase-163), followed by a within-model check of the DEDR entry, the positional profile, and phonotactics (Phase-166), in which a `strong' reference count means at least 4 hits --- a level M198 (3 hits) does not reach. All four signs remain PROVISIONAL\_MEDIUM. The Phase-166 labels CONFIRMED (for M330, M165, M202) and PROVISIONAL (for M198) are that check's internal labels; its own note states that CONFIRMED means only that the reading is phonologically plausible and has literature support, not that it is a HIGH-confidence reading, and no sign's category was changed by them. PROVISIONAL\_MEDIUM readings are
\emph{included} within the MEDIUM count for coverage accounting but are flagged for
expert review before any HIGH-confidence promotion. They are listed separately in Section 3.27. The Phase-163 mining record does not specify the proximity window (how near a reading must appear to a sign's number to count as a hit), the OCR source and version for every mined text, or how ambiguous sign-number matches were resolved. The four promotions therefore rest on a count whose generating rule is only partly recorded; that is a further reason, beyond the absence of expert review, that all four signs remain PROVISIONAL\_MEDIUM.

\textbf{LOW (236 signs)}: Heuristic assignment only: - Positional
assignment (initial/medial/terminal class) - Phase-111 allograph
resolution (positional profile match to an existing anchor) - Not used
for coverage or ``H+M-candidate coverage'' computations

\textbf{NOT COUNTED} toward coverage: signs at LOW confidence.

The HIGH, MEDIUM, and LOW labels are rule-based categories defined by the criteria above. They are not calibrated probabilities: no measured error rate attaches to any label. The outcome of the criteria for each sign is recorded in the anchor table (\texttt{research/\allowbreak indus/\allowbreak anchor\_table.csv}, `Basis' column). The anchor table records one basis statement and one evidence-type label per sign. It does not record each §2.2 criterion as a separate pass/fail field, so questions of the form `which signs meet criterion X but not criterion Y' cannot always be answered from the records --- §2.4's SA bound is one place where that limit binds, and it is stated there. One limit of the DEDR criterion must be stated plainly. For each sign, the anchor table records only the DEDR candidate that was adopted. How many candidate readings were considered for a sign, which sound changes or reading transformations were allowed when matching, and which candidates were rejected were not recorded; and no multiple-comparison control across the roughly 390 signs and the Dravidian Etymological Dictionary was applied or recorded. A reader therefore cannot compute from our records how often a DEDR match of this quality would arise by chance. The checks that do bear on the wider risk are the corpus tests reported in this paper --- the Phase-107 held-out test (§2.4) and the Phase-146 and Phase-150 null results (§§3.11, 3.13) --- not a correction to the DEDR search itself. Three of the criteria above are stated at the level this program's records support and no further: the iconographic-match criterion has no written coding protocol, coder-blinding step, or inter-coder reliability figure on record; `cases' in the terminal-marker criterion means the sign's corpus tokens; and the lift ratio in the exclusivity criterion is computed over the corpus's motif distribution, whose per-motif denominators are in the Phase-132 record's working outputs rather than a published table. A reader cannot re-apply the iconographic or exclusivity criteria to a new sign from this paper alone. That limit applies to every HIGH sign, not only to the three discussed in §3.3: the audit file records the same evidence-type pair (iconographic + distributional) for all 75 HIGH signs, §3.3 prints selected rows for 12 of them, and no record gives a criterion-by-criterion pass/fail for any HIGH sign. A reader therefore cannot verify from this paper, or from the audit file, that any particular HIGH sign meets two of the three criteria in §2.2 as those criteria are worded; what can be verified is the recorded evidence-type pair.

\subsubsection{2.3 Positional Analysis}\label{positional-analysis}

For each sign, we compute: - \textbf{Positional profile}: fraction of
tokens in INITIAL / MEDIAL / TERMINAL / SOLO positions - \textbf{Bigram
collocates}: most frequent left and right neighbors - \textbf{L1
distance} between positional profiles of candidate allograph pairs

Positional analysis classifies signs by position only: INITIAL-dominant,
TERMINAL-dominant, or MEDIAL. The semantic labels this model attaches to
those classes --- INITIAL read as title prefixes or classifier-derived
words, TERMINAL as case suffixes or proper-name terminals, MEDIAL as
content words or personal-name syllables --- are the model's
interpretation of the positional classes, not outputs of the positional
statistics (§3.2).

The headline positional statistic (z = 10.3) comes from the Phase-134 F1 test. We fit the three-slot grammar model to the real corpus (coefficient of determination $R^2$ = 0.9916) and to 2,000 copies of the corpus in which the signs inside each inscription were shuffled (mean $R^2$ = 0.4385, standard deviation 0.05347 --- the Phase-134 record's values before rounding). The z-score is the distance between the real value and the shuffled mean, measured in standard deviations: z = (0.9916 $-$ 0.4385) / 0.05347 = 10.344, printed as 10.3. No shuffled copy reached the real value. The same Phase-134 suite also ran a held-out check (F7): positional classes fitted on 1,176 seals predicted the classes of signs on 494 held-out seals with 97.7\% agreement, so the grammar model was not only fitted and scored on the same inscriptions. The F7 check behind the 97.7\% figure (Phase-134) is: positional classes fitted on 1,176 seals, tested on 494 held-out seals, over the 87 signs attested in both parts; 85 of those 87 signs were assigned the same dominant class in both parts (INITIAL 64/64, MEDIAL 5/5, MIXED 13/15, TERMINAL 3/3), which is the 97.7\% printed above. The F7 split was by site, not a random selection of seals: the positional classes were fitted on the seals of the three largest sites (Mohenjo-daro 606, Harappa 492, Chanhu-daro 78 --- 1,176 seals in total) and tested on the seals of the remaining six sites (494). The rule is in the Phase-134 script (\texttt{backend/scripts/phase134\_falsification\_suite.py}); the record's `80/20' label for the split is approximate --- the actual shares are 70.4\% and 29.6\% --- and no random seed is involved, because no random choice was made. Because the split is by site, the check tests whether classes fitted at the large sites predict the classes of signs at other sites; it is not a random held-out sample of seals. The corpus contains no single-sign inscriptions (Phase-134, test F9: 0 single-sign seals), so the SOLO positional class arises only as a profile category, not from one-sign texts. The permutation check behind z = 10.3 used 2,000 shuffled copies (§2.3); the Phase-134 record gives the count and the null moments but does not record the random seed or the shuffling code's inclusion rules, so the permutation cannot be re-run exactly from the record. The operative definition of this statistic --- what the grammar model fits, over which observations the R² is computed, and how inscriptions of different lengths enter the fit --- is the Phase-134 script (\texttt{backend/scripts/phase134\_falsification\_suite.py}). The Phase-134 JSON record reports the statistic's inputs and outputs (observed R², null mean and standard deviation, permutation count) but not the data matrix, and this section does not reproduce the script's procedure. The z-score can therefore be checked arithmetically from the printed inputs in this section (observed $R^2$ = 0.9916, null mean 0.4385, standard deviation 0.05347), but the fit itself can be re-run only from the script and the corpus.

\subsubsection{2.4 Syllabic Language Model (SA
Simulation)}\label{syllabic-language-model-sa-simulation}

For signs without iconographic anchors, we run a simulation (SA: a syllabic language model fitted by simulated annealing) trained on Tamil/Dravidian syllable sequences, and propagate readings through the sign graph. The SA assigns a modal reading (most probable syllable) and a consistency score (fraction of contexts agreeing on the reading). Signs with SA consistency \textgreater{}= 0.15 and a DEDR entry matching the modal reading were promoted to MEDIUM. Two limits must be stated plainly. The 0.15 threshold is a working cut-off chosen by this program; it has never been calibrated against a known script, so its false-positive rate is unknown. And a later test inside this program (Phase-107, October 2026) put the SA machinery itself to a held-out test whose protocol, primary metric, and discrimination rule were fixed in advance in this program's Spec 005 (\texttt{specs/005-phase52-v2/spec.md}), recorded in this repository before the runs; no external or public registration of that test was filed. Its agreement on held-out anchors was 0.000, and control runs using a Sanskrit model and a scrambled model could not be told apart from it. On that record, SA agreement by itself carries no information about a sign's value. MEDIUM readings whose only support is SA consistency should therefore be read as unconfirmed candidates; the provenance of every anchor, including which readings have SA in their chain of support, is recorded in the repository (Phase-108 provenance register; Phase-109 re-review). How much of the headline set this affects can be bounded from the audit file. No sign in the 161-set rests on SA alone: every sign whose evidence involves SA also carries the combined SA+DEDR evidence type. 43 MEDIUM signs, accounting for 398 corpus tokens, carry that combined evidence type in \texttt{ANCHOR\_STATUS\_AUDIT.csv}. The label records that a DEDR match was part of each sign's promotion route; it is not a count of entries in that file's DEDR column, where 15 of the 43 signs carry an entry and 28 do not. If all 43 were removed, 118 signs and 5,971 tokens (85.28\%) would remain. That is a bound, not a recomputed headline: the audit file does not record whether any of the 43 also meets a non-SA MEDIUM criterion (a positional profile or allograph support), so a true SA-free headline cannot be computed from the records. The 161 / 90.96\% headline therefore stands as the figure for the set as constituted, with this bound beside it. One part of the SA+DEDR criterion cannot be reconstructed from our records: the `consistency' score is described in the Phase-52 lineage as the fraction of a sign's contexts agreeing on its modal reading, but no record defines how a context is delimited, how contexts are weighted, or how ties are handled. The 0.15 threshold's status is as stated above: a working cut-off, never calibrated. What trained the SA must also be stated at the strength the records support. The records describe its training data only as Tamil/Dravidian syllable sequences, through the Phase-52 lineage of scripts and language-model files. No record we hold names a specific training corpus, a syllable-inventory source, or the size of the training data, so none is stated here: the SA's training set cannot be reconstructed from this paper or from the phase records.

\subsubsection{3.2 Grammar Model}\label{grammar-model}

Statistical analysis of inscription structure identifies a three-slot pattern that is consistent at the level of individual signs' positions. Whole inscriptions match the full pattern less often: in the Phase-133 check, 5.1\% of seals matched fully and 38.4\% partially (§3.31). `Consistent' in this paper refers to the sign-level regularity unless a sentence says otherwise:

\begin{verbatim}
[SLOT 1: INITIAL] – [SLOT 2: MEDIAL] – [SLOT 3: TERMINAL]
\end{verbatim}

Positional statistics establish the three positions and which signs favour each. The meanings attached to the positions below are this model's interpretation, not outputs of the statistics: we interpret Slot 1 as an animal classifier (from the seal motif), Slot 2 as a guild or group title, and Slot 3 as a personal-name ending and case marker. The pattern was tested against inscriptions with their signs shuffled; it was not tested against rival templates such as owner marks, commodity marks, or clan emblems, which could also produce fixed position patterns.

\textbf{Slot 1 (INITIAL)}: Examples: - Unicorn seals: M211=kol (lord/unicorn, from
kōl+rebus) - Zebu bull seals: M062=erutu (bull/ox), M073=kōṉ (king/bull)
- Elephant seals: M045=yānai, M039=āṉai - Rhinoceros seals: M060=kāṇṭāmirukam (a descriptive compound gloss, `rhinoceros'; no DEDR entry is recorded for this sign --- its support is iconographic and distributional only, 20 tokens)

\textbf{Slot 2 (MEDIAL)}: Examples: - M099=kol/koḷ (vessel/jar,
guild-of-vessels) - M233=ūr (settlement, of-the-town) - M162=il/iḷ
(house/dwelling) - M261=muruku (young man; 2 tokens only, from Parpola 1994 via this program's Phase-51 --- the further gloss `deity Murugan / guild-of-Murugan' is not supported by anything in our records and is withdrawn)

\textbf{Slot 3 (TERMINAL)}: Examples: - M342=ay/ā (genitive terminal, most frequent terminal at 584
tokens) - M176=an/aṇ (masculine suffix) - M367=am (neuter suffix) -
M336=iṉ (locative case marker)

Under this interpretation, an inscription would encode `the [animal-totem guild title] [person's name]'. The model predicts the \emph{position} of the name element only; it does not read any person's name (§3.9, §4.3). The pattern is comparable in function to a medieval European guild seal identifying a craftsman by occupation and personal name --- an analogy, not evidence.

\subsubsection{3.4 Major Correction: M267 Is Not the Fish Sign}\label{major-correction-m267-not-fish}

Version 6 of the anchor table assigned M267 (corpus frequency=400) as
``mīn/fish.'' This was incorrect. M267 appears across all nine motif types with no iconographic specificity. The counts are the Phase-132 record's (test V03 --- the distribution behind the chi-square printed below): unicorn 147 tokens, zebu bull 78, elephant 43, rhinoceros 26, buffalo 20, script only 34, geometric 19, tiger 17, gharial 16 --- 400 tokens in total, the sign's full corpus frequency. (Counts printed in earlier drafts --- unicorn 127, zebu 72, elephant 37, rhinoceros 25 --- came from the anchor file's basis note, do not match the test record's table, and are withdrawn; the chi-square below was never computed from them.) It is distributed at
5.7\% of all tokens. The actual fish sign is M047 (frequency=13, P47 in
Parpola's system), verified by crosswalk. M267 is a high-frequency functional/suffixal sign. Of its 400 occurrences, 324 (81.0\%) are MEDIAL, 59 TERMINAL, and 17 INITIAL; its most frequent right-hand neighbour is M099 (84 occurrences), which is the basis for describing its distribution as pre-title. Its distribution across seal motifs does not differ significantly from the counts expected if its 400 tokens were spread across the nine motif types in proportion to each type's overall share (chi-square = 12.98, p = 0.1124, 9 motif categories, 8 degrees of freedom; the full nine-motif count table is in the Phase-132 record, \texttt{backend/reports/phase132\_validation\_report.json}, test V03), which is why a motif-bound reading such as `fish' fails and a grammatical-particle reading is preferred. (`Uniform' in the Phase-149 summary of this test is that record's shorthand for the same expected-count comparison.) Its current MEDIUM reading is iN/in (genitive particle). One limit of this test belongs on the record: the chi-square treats M267's 400 tokens as independent observations, but a motif is a property of a seal, not of a token. The Phase-132 record does not state how many distinct seals the 400 tokens occur on, and no seal-level version of this test is on record, so the printed p-value should be read as a token-level result whose seal-level strength has not been measured. This correction is methodologically important: it demonstrates the risk of reading frequency alone as a phonetic indicator. The same warning applies to our own grammatical labels for M342 and M176 (§3.3), which also rest substantially on position and frequency and are stated there as candidates, not identifications.

\subsubsection{3.5 Fish Sign Isolation
 Test}\label{fish-sign-isolation-test}

The fish-sign model this test was built on is Avishai Roif's,
published in Roif (2025a, 2025b). In his model the fish sign is read
/mi/ (m\={i}n, ``fish/star'') as a value and divine marker and as a
guild/title marker (Roif 2025a); in the expanded treatment the
fish sign's holder is a ``coastal controller'' --- a maritime
guild leader --- and the fish, boat, and jar signs recur
predominantly at port sites, including Lothal (Roif 2025b). The
predictions this program tested --- a fish sign that functions as
an initial/title marker and is never isolated, and fish signs as
maritime markers rather than solitary quantity markers --- are
Roif's published predictions. The measurements reported in this
section and in \S3.19 (the counts, site tables, and verdicts) are
this program's own, from our corpus work: we do not attribute
our statistics to Roif, and his model is not ours.

What it establishes is a fact about this corpus --- 0 isolated fish signs among its 113 fish-bearing formal stamp seals --- and, because the corpus is one private collection of formal stamp seals only, not yet a fact about the Indus script at large, and, for the reason given above, it is not evidence of a fish-specific rule. A word on what 0 can and cannot show at these sizes. The bounds remain correct as arithmetic for a corpus in which single-sign inscriptions occur. `Site-invariant' in this paper therefore means only that no site showed an exception --- not that every site's rate is tightly measured.

A separate, weaker observation concerns pictures rather than script: Dilmun-style seals at Failaka Island (Hojlund \& Abu-Laban 2012) show fish in compound \emph{pictorial} contexts (human holding fish, fish attached to staffs), never as a solitary motif. This program's records do not tie the observation to specific catalogue numbers, figures, or pages in Hojlund \& Abu-Laban (2012); it is reported as a general observation from that volume, not as a checked count. Pictorial motifs are not Indus graphemes, and this is motif-level context only, not script evidence.

\subsubsection{3.7 Collocate Network and Formula Structure
(Phase-142)}\label{collocate-network-and-formula-structure-phase-142}

From the full corpus (1,670 seals, 7,002 tokens), we extracted 2,647 distinct bigrams --- adjacent sign pairs within inscriptions, in reading order --- including 1,485 H+M-by-H+M bigrams; the full table is the supplemental file \texttt{formula\_bigram\_table.csv}. The dominant formula backbone is \textbf{M342·M176} (\emph{ay/ā}·\emph{an/aṇ}), which appears on 122 seals (7.3\% of corpus, PMI=2.43) --- the highest-frequency H+M-by-H+M bigram pair. (It is not the highest-PMI pair in the corpus; the highest PMI values belong to rare pairs with counts of 3--5.) The next cluster is M267·M099
(\emph{iN/in}·\emph{kol/koḷ}, 84 seals), M099·M342 (81 seals), and
M176·M099 (74 seals). 97 distinct formula types are identified, organised by INITIAL sign.

Two terms used in this section are defined here. PMI is pointwise mutual information, in bits: $PMI(a, b) = \log_2 [ P(a, b) / (P(a) \cdot P(b)) ]$, where P(a, b) is the pair's share of all bigram tokens and P(a) and P(b) are the two signs' shares of all sign tokens (Phase-142 script, \texttt{backend/scripts/phase142\_collocate\_network.py}). For the worked example in this section: M342·M176 occurs 122 times; M342 accounts for 584 of the corpus's 7,002 sign tokens and M176 for 356 (§3.3); the pair's share is taken over all bigram tokens in the Phase-142 record: 5,332 bigram tokens --- the corpus's 7,002 sign tokens minus one for each of its 1,670 inscriptions. For this example, P(M342·M176) = 122/5,332, P(M342) = 584/7,002 and P(M176) = 356/7,002, and the printed PMI of 2.43 recomputes from those three fractions (\texttt{backend/reports/phase142\_collocate\_network.json}), which is also where the distinct-bigram counts above (2,647 in total; 1,485 H+M-by-H+M) are recorded. PMI values from this section use sign-token marginals, as defined above, and are not comparable to bigram-marginal PMI values in other work. A `formula type' is one INITIAL sign together with the seals whose inscriptions begin with it: the 97 types are the 97 distinct INITIAL signs that begin inscriptions in this corpus and carry a HIGH or MEDIUM reading (Phase-142 formula classification; the clusters built on them are Phase-148's) --- which is why §3.12's clusters are organised by INITIAL sign. A seal belongs to a type by its first sign alone; its other signs need not carry HIGH or MEDIUM readings. Every seal in the corpus begins with one of these 97 signs, so the 97 types together cover all 1,670 seals --- not only the 1,165 seals that consist entirely of HIGH-or-MEDIUM signs (§3.6).

Positional constraint test (Phase-142D) --- descriptive result only: in the raw counts, 17 of the 21 tested H+M signs have different collocate sets when they stand in INITIAL position from when the same signs stand in non-INITIAL positions. Whether that difference is more than chance is what the permutation test in §3.13 was for; its verdict was INCONCLUSIVE. The 17/21 figure is therefore a description of the counts, not evidence for or against position-specific collocates, and we do not use it as support for the grammar model. M267 is the strongest single case on the descriptive numbers: 324 of its 400 occurrences (81.0\%) are MEDIAL (§3.4).

\subsubsection{3.14 Site Repertoire Divergence Bootstrap
(Phase-151)}\label{site-repertoire-divergence-bootstrap-phase-151}

In this section, KL divergence compares two sites' sign repertoires: each site is represented by the distribution of its H+M sign tokens over the signs attested at that site. The measure is symmetric KL --- half the sum of the two one-way divergences, $D_{KL}(P|Q)$ and $D_{KL}(Q|P)$ --- in bits (log base 2), with Laplace smoothing (one count added for every sign in the pair's combined vocabulary), as defined in the Phase-151 script (\texttt{backend/scripts/phase151\_site\_kl\_bootstrap.py}). The bootstrap resamples seals with replacement within each site, 1,000 resamples (the Phase-151 script fixes the random seed at 42); a confidence interval was computed for the most divergent pair only. Neither the Phase-151 record nor its script names the interval method (percentile, basic, or bias-corrected), so the printed interval is reported as recorded, without a method name. The KL divergence in §3.10 is a different quantity: a one-directional divergence, $D_{KL}(\text{Holdat}|\text{CISI})$, over the four positional-class shares, with 0.001 added to each share (Phase-144/145 script). The 36 observed site-pair values in this section are descriptive; intervals are printed for one pair only, so no family of interval claims is made.

Bootstrap confidence intervals (n=1,000 resamples) were computed for the most divergent pair only. Key findings: Rakhigarhi (n=33) has the largest observed divergence against every other site in this corpus. All 36 observed site-pair values are in the Phase-151 record (\texttt{backend/reports/phase151\_site\_kl\_bootstrap.json}); the largest is Mohenjo-daro--Rakhigarhi (0.509), and the next largest is also a Rakhigarhi pair (Harappa--Rakhigarhi, 0.481), so the `most divergent pair' named below can be checked against that record. Intervals were computed for that one pair only, so the ordering of the other pairs is descriptive, not interval-tested.
A bootstrap confidence interval was computed for the most divergent pair: Mohenjo-daro vs Rakhigarhi KL=0.509, 95\% CI [0.483, 0.750]. The other Rakhigarhi pairs have observed KL values between 0.250 and 0.481 (Harappa 0.481, Kalibangan 0.350, Lothal 0.348, Dholavira 0.320, Chanhu-daro 0.291, Surkotada 0.290, Banawali 0.250); no confidence intervals for those pairs are on record, and the earlier draft's claim that five Rakhigarhi comparisons all have interval lower bounds above 0.3 is withdrawn. The large-site pair (Mohenjo-daro vs Harappa) shows KL=0.070 --- a small measured difference between the two repertoires. A shared scribal tradition is one possible explanation; corpus composition and object-type selection were not controlled for, and could produce the same small difference. Rakhigarhi has only 33 seals; resampling those 33 seals cannot add information about the site, so its distinctiveness should be read as a measured difference on a small sample, not as proof that the difference is not a small-sample effect.

\subsubsection{3.17 Vowel Harmony Methodology Resolution
(Phase-154)}\label{vowel-harmony-methodology-resolution-phase-154}

The V12 vowel-harmony warning is unresolved, and our own records disagree about it. Depending on the version of the test, the pass rate is 75.3\% (Phase-132: 476 of 632 decoded seals, against an 85\% threshold), 64.2\% on a stricter cross-reading version (Phase-133e), or 36.1\% (Phase-154: 417 of the 1,154 seals checked \emph{passed}; 737 seals failed), where the recorded verdict is V12\_ACTIVE\_CONCERN. The 418 figure in that record is a count of word-internal (root) violations, not a count of seals --- a different unit, close to the 417 passing seals only by coincidence: 418 root violations plus 319 boundary violations are the violations counted across the 737 failing seals. That record's 418 root violations led it to suggest that some sign readings have the wrong vowel class. Two known sources of false violations exist --- slash-notation alternative readings (e.g., ``ay/ā'') and grammatical particles that follow words of any vowel class --- but they have not been shown to account for the Phase-154 result. We therefore withdraw the earlier description of V12 as a settled methodology artifact: vowel harmony is an open problem for the reading set, and root harmony within single morphemes is in any case not testable with the current toolkit without morphological segmentation. No one of these three procedures is currently the program's authoritative V12 test: the records disagree because the test was redefined between runs, and the full rule changes are not recorded for every version. The V12 warning therefore stands as unresolved at every strength the three versions give (pass rates 75.3\%, 64.2\% and 36.1\%), and no reading in this paper is either cleared or condemned by V12 until one protocol is frozen and re-run on the current 161-sign set.

\subsubsection{3.31 Grammar Variance Retest at 161 H+M

Retest of the Phase-133 grammar explained variance metric with the
updated 161 H+M anchor set (adds M330, M165, M202, M198). Method:
for each H+M sign with \textgreater{}=3 corpus tokens (147/161 qualify), compute
I/M/T positional rates and test whether the 3-slot grammar model
(TERMINAL if T\textgreater{}=0.60, INITIAL if I\textgreater{}=0.50, MEDIAL if M\textgreater{}=0.65)
correctly classifies the sign's dominant positional class. The Phase-170 record states the rules in the order TERMINAL, INITIAL, MEDIAL, else MIXED; it does not state what happens when a sign's rates satisfy more than one threshold, and that tie/overlap handling is not on record.

\subsection{4. Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsubsection{4.1 Proposed Readings Under the Candidate Model}\label{proposed-readings-candidate-model}

The three sequences below are illustrative sequences, built from the model's readings to show how the grammar is meant to be read. They are sign-number sequences; this paper does not tie them to identified, catalogued seals, and they should not be cited as transcriptions of specific seals.

Under the grammar model, a typical seal reads as:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{M211 M099 M342} = \emph{kol-kol-ay} = ``the jar-guild lord''
  (unicorn seal, formal guild title)
\item
  \textbf{M062 M342 M176} = \emph{erutu-ay-an} = ``the bull's man''
  (zebu bull seal, personal name)
\item
  \textbf{M045 M176 M267 M328} = \emph{yānai-an-iN-āl} = ``the elephant
  guild man's man'' (compound title)
\end{itemize}

Under the model --- and only under the model --- these inscriptions read as a directory of guild members rather than a commodity ledger; the two readings have not been separated by any test yet (§4.5 names a future one), and rival functions (ownership, clan, cultic, or commodity marking) are not ruled out. The translations above carry the uncertainty of every reading they use; the first, for example, depends on two different signs (M211 and M099) both being read kol. On repeated readings, the rule of this model is: homophony is permitted --- two different signs may carry the same reading --- whenever each sign's own evidence supports that reading, as with M211 and M099 here. Shared readings are common in the 161-set, but no audited count of them is on record, and none is claimed. The Arthaśāstra (a layered Sanskrit text whose core is usually placed in the last centuries BCE, roughly a millennium and a half after the end of the IVC period) documents Sanskrit superintendent titles of a broadly similar kind --- \emph{koṣṭhāgārādhyakṣa} (storehouse superintendent), \emph{nāvadhyakṣa} (ship superintendent). No word is shared with the proposed Dravidian readings, and the parallel is an analogy about administrative practice, not evidence of continuity, which remains speculative.

\subsubsection{4.2 The Substrate Evidence}\label{the-substrate-evidence}

Two signs (M374=kul, M351=vī, six tokens each) are assigned DEDR-backed candidate readings under the model, for which a Munda derivation was proposed but not established (see §3.3 for the limits of both; the recorded basis is DEDR 1709 and DEDR 5388, not substrate evidence). We record, as speculation and nothing more, the scenario they would fit if they were correct: a linguistically mixed IVC in which a Dravidian-writing administration absorbed loanwords from Munda-speaking communities. Two candidate signs cannot establish that scenario, and no claim about an administrative elite or about community structure is made on their basis. The Munda substrate in historical
Dravidian and Indo-Aryan languages is well-attested (Witzel 1999;
Southworth 2005). If the proposed readings are correct, this would suggest that such substrate influence predates later attested Munda vocabulary.

\subsubsection{4.3 The 18 Signs Resisting MEDIUM Promotion}\label{the-18-signs-resisting-medium}

Eighteen signs with corpus frequency 5--6 resist MEDIUM promotion under current methodology. Their modals
from the syllabic LM are syllables without clear DEDR matches (e.g.,
`o', `e', `pi', `du', `pit'). One reading of this pattern, which the model adopts, is that these signs are syllabic components of personal names --- in a system where personal-name syllables could be arbitrary, they would not be recoverable from distributional statistics alone. The statistics do not distinguish that reading from any other medial-only sign class (§3.9 above), so it is a preference of the model, not a finding. The limit stated here belongs to these distributional statistics, not to method in general: other evidence (repeated name sequences, external name matches) could separate the two, and none is available in this corpus.

\subsubsection{4.4 Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{No bilingual text}: All readings remain provisional until a bilingual inscription (IVS + Sumerian, Akkadian, or Sanskrit) is discovered. No numerical probability attaches to any individual reading; the confidence labels are rule-based categories (§2.2).
\item
  \textbf{Corpus size}: 7,002 tokens is small for linguistic reconstruction. High-frequency signs have more positional data than low-frequency signs, but frequency has not made their readings secure: the V07 phonotactic violations are concentrated in high-frequency headline signs (§3.18), and the program's largest single correction was to a 400-token sign (§3.4). Low-frequency signs (the irresolvable 18) have too little data for either kind of check.
\item
  \textbf{Single corpus}: The Holdat corpus covers formal stamp seals
  only. Copper tablets, potsherd graffiti, and the Dholavira signboard
  require separate analysis.
\item
  \textbf{SA simulation}: The syllabic language model is described in this program's records as trained on modern Tamil material, but no record names the specific corpus, the syllable-inventory source, or the size of the training data, so the training set cannot be reconstructed (§2.4). Phonological drift over 4,000 years may introduce systematic errors, and the size of that risk cannot be assessed without the training specification.
\item
  \textbf{Confidence standards}: The MEDIUM threshold is a rule, not a measured standard: it has no known false-positive rate (§2.2), and it admits routes --- SA+DEDR agreement and, for four signs, literature text proximity --- whose error rates are likewise unmeasured. Some assigned LOW readings may be correct but lack sufficient support under the rule.
\item
  \textbf{Language-family baseline}: The present model tests Proto-Dravidian
  compatibility more extensively than competing baselines. A formal comparison
  against Proto-Munda, early Indo-Aryan, and language-neutral syllabic models
  remains necessary before the linguistic interpretation can be treated as discriminative rather than merely compatible with Proto-Dravidian. For the record, the comparisons that do exist are mixed: a syllabic language-model comparison favoured Dravidian over Sanskrit on the 157-sign set (Phase-134 F12), while a phonological-profile comparison of the HIGH anchors found Dravidian and Sanskrit fits equal and rated the question AMBIGUOUS (Phase-134 F3).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{4.5 Validation Path}\label{validation-path}

The candidate model is falsifiable in principle at the first of the following points. None of the three is an open test that can still be run on the data available today: the first requires a future discovery, the second has already been run and did not confirm the model, and the third has no discriminating prediction on record yet. The three points are stated so that a reader can see exactly what would have to happen, and what has already happened, at each. 1. \textbf{Discovery of
a bilingual text}: Any Indus inscription alongside a known script
(Sumerian, Akkadian, Elamite) at a Gulf trade site would allow direct
validation of individual sign readings. 2. \textbf{Gulf material} (Phase-156, now complete): the Laursen (2010) Gulf Type seal catalog text and the Mitchell (1986) Bahrain volume text were searched. The search found no mention of an isolated fish sign in either text, and no fish-sign mentions at all in the Laursen text (§3.19 gives the source-specific statement), so the Gulf material provides no counterexample to the compound-only pattern --- and no confirmation of it either (§3.19). No verdict on a fish-specific compound-only rule is claimed: the Holdat count is 0 isolated among 113 fish-bearing seals in a corpus with no single-sign inscriptions at all (§3.5), and no extended verdict across both corpora is claimed. 3.
\textbf{Radiocarbon-dated stratigraphic contexts}: a future test of this kind could separate the guild-identity interpretation from the commodity-tally model only if the two are first made to predict different things --- for example, different sign-sequence patterns in seals from a single dated layer. Consistent `guild' readings in one layer, on their own, would presuppose the readings and would support neither model over the other. No such discriminating prediction is yet on record, so this point is a direction for future work, not a falsification test of the present model.

\subsection{5. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

We propose a computational grammar and candidate Proto-Dravidian anchor
model for the Indus Valley Script. Its scripts, phase reports and anchor
table can be re-run and checked from the repository; its corpus-level
counts cannot be re-run until the Holdat corpus is released (§6). The strongest findings, which can be stated with high confidence, are two: robust non-random positional structure (\textit{z}\,=\,10.3; no permutation in 2,000 exceeded the observed statistic (p \textless{} 0.001; 0 of the 2,000 permutations exceeded the observed statistic, so the plain estimate is 0/2,000 and the standard add-one estimate is (0+1)/(2,000+1) = 0.0005, the smallest non-zero value a test of this size can report) --- §2.3 defines the statistic), and the correction of the M267 misassignment (M267 is not the fish sign: it occurs across all motif types, §3.4). Two further structural results are stated at the strength their tests support: the fish-sign isolation count in the Holdat corpus (0 isolated among 113 fish-bearing seals --- a count that follows from the corpus's containing no single-sign inscriptions at all, and so is not a fish-specific pattern, §3.5; the Gulf extension is weaker, §3.19), and a recurring three-slot positional pattern whose positional classes are measured but whose semantic labels (classifier, title, name) are this model's interpretation (§3.2). The tier labels used in this conclusion are this program's claim-audit labels (\texttt{CLAIM\_AUDIT.md}), defined here because the paper uses them without defining them elsewhere: Tier 2 means a probable linguistic interpretation --- supported, not proven; Tier 3, used below, means speculative or provisional, requiring explicit caveats. The candidate Proto-Dravidian readings (161 H+M signs; 90.96\% token coverage --- assignment coverage in the sense of §2.2: the share of tokens whose sign carries a candidate reading, not a share of verified readings) are Tier~2 claims supported by multiple tests. Those tests are not independent of each other --- most are computed from the same corpus, and several from the same seals (§2.2) --- the HIGH/MEDIUM labels are rule-based categories with no measured error rate, and the set also carries the unresolved V12 warning and the 23.4\% V07 phonotactic violation rate (§§3.17--3.18) --- a rate concentrated in high-frequency headline signs (M328, M211, M176 and M342 drive it, §3.18), not spread over rare or doubtful readings. The readings have not yet been externally reviewed. Speculative
interpretations --- guild-identity semantics, the two DEDR-backed readings for which a Munda derivation was proposed but not established,
individual seal translations --- are Tier~3 claims requiring expert
review and explicit caveats.

This study does not claim epigraphic finality in the absence of a
bilingual inscription. The corpus shows non-random positional structure of the kind a writing system produces and also of the kind some non-linguistic marking systems produce; the tests in this paper do not separate those two, and the frequency-curve evidence that might have spoken to the question was uninformative (§2.1.1). Within that limit, the evidence favours a Proto-Dravidian reading framework under the tested assumptions. The ICIT corpus
is the immediate
priority for testing whether the candidate model generalises to a larger public corpus. All phase
reports, scripts, and the anchor table are available in the Glossa-Lab repository.
